Synopsis
Disorders of Gut-Brain Interaction (DGBI), such as Irritable Bowel Syndrome, are highly prevalent and debilitating.
While therapeutic diets like the low FODMAP diet are first-line treatment and effective for short-term symptom control,
they frequently lead to long-term, maladaptive over-restriction, causing nutritional deficiencies, psychological distress,
and diminished quality of life.

A critical gap exists in clinical practice: there are no validated tools to identify patients at risk of this harmful overrestriction, nor are there evidence-based protocols to guide them back to a more liberalised diet.

This research program directly addresses this gap by seeking to establish a new, evidence-based framework for safe
dietary liberalisation.
The program is comprised of four integrated studies:
1) A cross-sectional study to identify the clinical and behavioural predictors of over-restriction and develop a clinical
risk-stratification tool.
2) A longitudinal study to prospectively validate this tool and characterise the psychological drivers of persistent food
avoidance.
3) A randomised controlled trial to test the efficacy of a novel, integrated dietetic and cognitive-behavioural therapy
(CBT) intervention for safely increasing dietary variety in high-risk patients.
4) A pilot study to evaluate the feasibility of implementing this new care model into a real-world multidisciplinary clinic
pathway.

The outputs of this research—a validated risk score, a manualised intervention, and an implementation-tested clinical
pathway—will equip clinicians with the tools to shift DGBI management from a focus on restriction to one that
balances symptom control with nutritional and psychological wellbeing, directly informing future clinical practice and
guidelines.

Plain English Summary
People with gut disorders often avoid foods to manage symptoms, but this can become overly restrictive, harming
their health and creating food anxiety. We will identify which patients are most at risk. We will then test if combining
dietitian support with psychological therapy to reduce food-related fear is the most effective way to help them
reintroduce foods safely. The findings will create a new team-based care model, allowing patients to expand their diet
and improve their overall wellbeing

Over the past five years, I have transformed the care of disorders of gut-brain interaction (DGBI) from a fragmented,
symptom-based approach into an internationally endorsed, multidisciplinary model that improves health, saves
money, and elevates community awareness. The key study was the MANTRA randomised controlled trial, which I
conceived and led to publication in The Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology (1). Its findings now inform guideline
recommendations on three continents.
REACH & SIGNIFICANCE OF THE IMPACT
Knowledge impact – MANTRA provided the first high-quality evidence that coordinated input from gastroenterologists,
psychiatrists, physiotherapists, and dietitians outperforms routine specialist care for DGBIs (1). The paper (1) has
accumulated >112 citations (FWCI 3.96, FCR 27), secured the 2021 Ray Clouse Award (2) as the world’s best
publication in this field, and underpins updated IBS/DGBI guidelines issued by the British Society of Gastroenterology
(3), Indian Society of Gastroenterology (4), and the Belgian Association of Gastroenterology (5). Follow-up in Clinical
Gastroenterology & Hepatology (6) confirmed durable benefits 12 months after therapy, cementing a paradigm shift
toward multidisciplinary, biopsychosocial management. Complementary systematic reviews that I directed have
mapped service gaps and quantified the overlooked burden of disordered eating in GI clinics (7)—data now
referenced in the American Psychiatric Association Practice Guideline on the Treatment of Patients with Eating
Disorders (8) and driving research into eating-GI comorbidity.
Health impact – Using the model of care validated by the MANTRA study, I established a comprehensive, publicly
funded multidisciplinary DGBI clinic at St Vincent’s Hospital Melbourne (2019). The model has since been copied by
≥three tertiary centres nationally and one internationally. Service audits documented an average 57% reduction in
symptom severity (9). We also saw a complete elimination of inappropriate artificial-feeding referrals for patients with
DGBI.
My leadership on national steering committees is embedding the same principles into guidelines for eosinophilic
oesophagitis (2025), gastroparesis (10), and complex disordered eating, extending impact to new patient groups.
Economic impact – A health-economic analysis undertaken alongside MANTRA demonstrated an incremental costeffectiveness ratio of AU$3,555 per QALY, well below internationally accepted willingness-to-pay thresholds (1).
Patients managed in the multidisciplinary pathway at longer-term follow-up recorded 25% fewer physician visits, a tenfold reduction in repeat colonoscopies, and markedly lower use of high-cost imaging (6). The model is projected to
save significant healthcare costs annually through reduced investigations and productivity gains from reduced work
absenteeism(6).
Social impact – By collaborating with Dr Reddy’s Pharmaceuticals, I was able to translate the MANTRA study into a
smartphone application (11) in India. Media features in ABC Online (12), MedPage Today (13) , and the University of
Melbourne Pursuit (14) have reframed DGBIs as treatable disorders of gut–brain interaction rather than vague “IBS,”
reducing stigma and improving help-seeking. I co-authored the 2022 Better Health Channel IBS page (15) (the most
visited Victorian consumer health site).
MY CONTRIBUTION TO REALISING, SUSTAINING & MAXIMISING IMPACT
From the outset, I positioned translation as the primary aim of my research, proactively ensuring each discovery would
rapidly evolve into clinical practice, policy, and medical education.
Proactive & Deliberate Leadership
In June 2018, while completing my PhD, I secured >AU $160 000 from the Victorian Medical Research Acceleration
Fund to pilot feasibility studies of faecal microbiota transplantation in Crohn’s disease. Those data underpinned the
MIRO 2 randomised controlled trial—comparing FMT versus standard care in Crohn’s—which I now co-supervise.
For my multidisciplinary model of care in FGIDs, I personally authored the MANTRA trial design, embedding rigorous
health-economic evaluations, and patient-reported outcomes from the outset.
Following initial publication in The Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology (2020) (1), I proactively led the long-term
follow-up study (published in Clinical Gastroenterology & Hepatology, 2022) (6) to robustly assess the durability and
sustainability of multidisciplinary outcomes. I also initiated and led systematic reviews on DGBI care delivery
models(16) and the prevalence of disordered eating in gastrointestinal populations(7), providing critical foundational
context that identified key knowledge gaps and helped guide targeted future research and clinical development.

Research Programs & Team Leadership

gastroenterologists, dieticians, psychiatrists, physiotherapists and specialist nurses in a single, research-enabled care
pathway. The clinic’s efficacy was formally demonstrated in the MANTRA randomised controlled trial—the only RCT to
compare multidisciplinary care with standard gastroenterologist-led care in functional gut disorders—published in
Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology and followed by long-term outcomes in Clinical Gastroenterology &
Hepatology. As Chief Investigator, I led the multidisciplinary steering committee, oversaw protocol development, data
collection and analysis, and mentored trainee-led substudies in qualitative outcomes and health-economics analyses.

Institutional Leadership
Over the past five years at St Vincent’s Hospital Melbourne, I have championed behavioural and cultural change to
embed research excellence into everyday clinical practice. In 2019, I co-founded the multidisciplinary functional gut
disorders clinic—bringing together gastroenterology, dietetics, physiotherapy, psychiatry and specialist nursing into a
unified model of care pathway. Following the MANTRA randomised controlled trial (published in Lancet
Gastroenterology & Hepatology), this model of integrated care has been adopted by multiple tertiary centres across
Australia and was recognised by the Rome Foundation with the Ray Clouse Award for Best Research Paper in
Functional Gut Disorders.
To address inequities in access to oesophageal motility diagnostics, I established Victoria’s busiest public
oesophageal physiology laboratory. I recruited and trained dedicated clinical staff, created formal referral pathways
from regional hospitals, and redesigned workflows to eliminate diagnostic backlogs—ensuring patients from
metropolitan and rural communities receive timely, high-quality testing.
Partnering with departmental leadership, I secured ongoing operational funding to expand our motility services. I then
implemented the prospective collection of validated, patient-reported symptom outcomes for everyone undergoing
oesophageal manometry: each patient completes a 60-item questionnaire while awaiting their test. That data
underpinned a study comparing oesophageal hypervigilance with manometry results as predictors of symptom
severity. These initiatives have shortened diagnostic delays and ensured more equitable access to care, fostering a
culture of continuous quality improvement across the department.
